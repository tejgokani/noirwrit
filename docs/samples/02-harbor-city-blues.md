## Chapter 1: The Call

They found Hugh Blount at a speakeasy on Front Street, and by the time I got there the rain had washed away most of what mattered. We put our heads together and talked it over--it was a long speech for me that night. Then we left the courthouse and started back to the city hall.

I will not dwell upon the details of the events of the day. The thing has gone straight with my mind, too. My brain was clear in its train of thought. I did not know where I should find my way, or in what part of the world I could find a place. And yet I dared not venture out of the city hall. I knew the men who were working for me, as well as those in the city hall, and I had come to the conclusion that they would be the victims of one of them.

All that night, as the sun was rising, I paced the streets in a fever of excitement and expectation. The afternoon dragged along. The night wore on. It was dusk when the next day, one of the first of the morning, we moved out into the country.

We walked for some distance. Just as we reached the edge of the woods, I saw that the road was deserted. A few yards farther, the side lights of a village showed in the distance. On the right a man was standing. The man's figure was hidden by a large maple tree and was silhouetted against the light in the direction from which he had emerged.

The man spoke. He said: "Yes," but seemed to speak to his thoughts.

"I wish you would take that maple tree."

"Why--why, it's the same thing."

He said: "It is the same thing. You are not here as a spy or newspaper man, are you?"

"No, I am not."

"Well, give it to me."

"I have told you," said the man. "You understand. When you go back to the city you will see your way to go there as far as the front door of the church. That will be before I return."

"What is the matter with the church? What do you mean?"

"I mean that I am here, on this track, where I have no interest, no interest, no interest, because you will be there to find it."

"Oho! You have found it!"

"Don't you know what I mean? Have you ever seen it before?"

"Never."

"Then you don't know what you are talking about?"

"Do you think I am keeping it hidden in my mind?"

"I'm sure of it."

"Have you never had any business here?"

"Yes; I have been away."

"Well, and what then?"

"You shall know."

"Wait."

The man rose slowly and walked back toward the village. I saw him walk out, pass on to the main road. He went along a little more before I took the oath.

When he was a hundred yards from me I came up to him, and said: "There's just one thing we need to do now. There's some sort of game on foot that we'll want to catch."

The client sat across from me and said the police had it wrong about Blount. Then he put the maple-glasses over his face again, and said: "But, say, you'll have to get right into the house first. I don't think I could help you much if I were to do so. If you don't like the way you talk, you will."

"What do you mean?" I asked.

"Why, if you don't want to be questioned, I'll tell you," he said. "You'll have to get there. You won't be able to make yourself heard. You'll have to wait. And in case you're not questioned, or anything of that kind, you can't tell me anything that would put the slightest color in your face. You can say that I'm dead and done with this."

I didn't say anything. The man turned his head away from me and walked slowly back to the village street. I looked after him for a moment. He was gone quite a while. Then I stood up and went out.

After an hour's walk I came in front of the hotel. I bought a ticket to the village. As I got out I heard steps on the stairs. I jumped for the door. The footsteps of the people stopped at the door. The steps stopped. It seemed as though they must have been waiting for somebody. There was no one in the room. I got into my shoes. I started in through the doorway. The hall lights were on. When I reached the top of the stairs, I saw that Blount was standing at the window of a room where Blount was.

As I stepped inside, I heard voices. A voice said: "There they are now."

I turned and ran along the hall to the window. The light from the fire-escape was burning brightly. I could see through the window. And I could see the light on the table top. I could see the face of a man there in the glow, but not the face of a girl, and it was dark.

"That's Blount," I said to myself. "And if he is right, Blount is the real Blount. What does he want? That would be a good plan to get him. He will do the rest. But what does he expect me to do?"

A telephone bell rang through the house. I tried to get the number of the hotel, but the words stuck on their way. They weren't very pleasant. I had to go to the door of the room where Blount was talking to some one who wanted to speak to her.

Then a voice came:

"Who's there? Who's here?"

I stood still. It was a woman's voice. There wasn't any answer.

"Come in!" I said to the doorman. "What's wrong?"

He came in and took my arm.

"They've been talking about something this morning," he explained, "and we're going out for a walk."

"Gone where?" I asked.

"To police headquarters - a private resort, you know."

"Where?" I asked.

"Westward - in the woods, I think."

Vance did not want to talk, but I had come a long way to the agency office and I was in no hurry to leave. The call came from the local police headquarters.

I left the hall door open behind me, and stepped inside the elevator. Then I heard a key turned in the lock of the door, and then a footstep on the stairs. It was dark now in front of the desk, but I knew it must be the one with which Blount had just been speaking. I went across the room and looked at the desk. It was covered with a white sheet of paper, and there were several letters, both addressed to Mr. Vance and in an envelope on the table before me. The ink was wet, and the writing was ink-well. I stepped over to the desk and read: "Mr. Vance has discovered that I am right."

A man, whom I recognized as Vance's private secretary, entered the room while I stood by the desk. He was tall, lanky, and, if I had ever seen him before, I should have known him at once, for he sat beside the desk, leaning against the desk. I saw his face distinctly - the thin lips were drawn back straight into an open, drawn line. He was youngish, very pale, and I couldn't tell whether he was handsome or not. His face was hard and set. As I stood there, staring, he spoke in a loud tone.

"This is my friend," he said quietly. "I want you to take charge of him at once. He will get away."

He put the letter down on the table, and came over and sat down at the desk. "My friend," he began, "is dead. You have no idea what he is up to?"

The question was a rather quick one, but I did not ask it of myself. I merely replied that I had never heard anything like this before. It struck me that perhaps it was a little too much for me to consider it seriously. The answer came slowly and painfully: "No," he answered, "I haven't any. But I am going to work for Mr. Vance's life. I do not know how long he has been lying here."

Then I could see that he was struggling with all he might say. "You will help me?" I asked. "Do you mean that you will help me?"

"It is not necessary that you should," he said; "it is necessary that you should. That is why I want you to promise that you shall notify the police. There are reasons why I can do that."

I saw now why the other was waiting to speak - the man who had just come into the room. He appeared to be in deadly earnest. Then he moved his hand and held it behind his back as if he were afraid to touch the desk.

"If you will tell me what my friend has done, I will tell you," he said, "and I will go out there and get your friend. But if you insist, I will ask you not to call the police or the police, either. You understand? I am not a coward. But I have a friend who knows what to expect from a stranger.

It was a glove for the left hand only, and I turned it over in my hand for a long time. But the fingers still held mine. They did not reach at once. The glove was gone.

The man whom I had seen go out into the hall was still talking to himself. He seemed to be looking at something in front of him, then down again on the floor beside him. His eyes were closed, his face drawn. He was like one turned to stone.

"You must leave this house at once, Mr. Vance," he said quietly. "I know where we are now."

I don't think I remembered the words that he had spoken - I don't remember them even. All I remember is that I have known them before. Then suddenly he leaned forward and lifted both hands high above his head. Then he held them up. He shook them as he spoke. He did not speak. It was like an echo. A strange smile flitted across his lips. "This is a pleasure I have never met with before."

He seemed to know that I would not let him. He spoke quite simply, but without the slightest attempt at accent. He gave me the impression that the other was saying: "Yes; that is what I thought. But there are still other things to do. I should like you to do it. My friend here, the police, will be interested in seeing you, I imagine, if you will stay here until I return. I shall be glad to see you later."

The man raised his hands above his head. "I am sorry, Mr. Vance, but I cannot go back."

Then he moved back across the room toward the door.

"And now?" he asked.

"It must be done," said the other. "I am going out now. I am going to find my friend again."

"Let me see him," said the other.

They were in front of the desk. They stood facing each other. The man turned as if to go, then walked slowly and heavily away. I could hear the quick breathing of the other. But I did not stir.

For a long minute I remained motionless. Then the other said: "That's all right, sir; that's all right. I haven't any use for you, though, or for you."

And then, as he got up from where he had been standing, he said: "You will have a chance to look about the house without leaving it, for I am sure you will find everything there you want. I do not know whether I am safe to trust you with anything I may do to-night."

He went out through the door at which I was sitting. As I stepped into the hall, I noticed that the light came from one window. It was lighted so that the room beyond could be seen.

I knocked twice. A servant entered.

"There is some one waiting to see you, sir," said the girl.

The servant appeared in answer to an imperative ring. He looked up and down the street. Then he said sharply: "Do you know why I asked you to bring the gentleman in?"

The servant replied that it was because he was going out. He put his hand on the man's shoulder.

I looked at Vance and said, "You were there that night." Then I told him what I had done, and he said: "The gentleman will come in here later, Mr. Blount." Then he went out of the house.

A few minutes later I heard the front door open and close. I walked out to the rear of the house, and saw the two men standing in the shadow near the street entrance. Then the other disappeared into a side street.

"Well, if I can't find the doctor who was murdered," said the tall, thin man, "it is a wonder he wasn't shot by somebody who didn't have a motive for killing him."

I went back to the house. I was afraid the doctor might not hear me. So I waited a little while before coming home. There was no one to be seen, and I had to wait till midnight. At midnight the same man came out of the house. I followed him to the door. He stood there a moment or two looking about the room. Then he stepped out and went down the stairs.

Then the dark-bearded man came out again and walked across the lawn toward the river. He sat down on the edge of the bank. He began to talk with animation, and his voice seemed to be a regularity of his voice.

As he talked, I could feel that he was talking to himself. The man looked at me curiously when he spoke, and then said: "You know what I mean, don't you?"

I told him what I meant, but said nothing as I was doing. He went on, however, without saying anything. When he left I went to the dining room to get the doctor's hat and overcoat. They were gone. I put them in my overcoat. He went away. I went to the window. As I stood there, I heard voices. I felt sure that somebody was trying to kill me. But I didn't understand. I moved quickly up the steps, opened the door softly, and went out. When I got back to the porch, I heard a door open and some one coming in. I jumped up and went to the porch. The doctor had come back. When I reached him he was gone.

When I returned to the house, I found that I had not been seen about the town. The police were there, and a messenger came back with the message: "Disguise."

Then I went home.

I have always thought that this way is the most foolish thing in the world. For instance, you never saw a newspaper, or heard one; did you? And you never knew it. You are a man who knows his own mind, and does not know his own mind. Don't talk to yourself any more about the way things happen. Just watch me, and I'll see what I mean."

Yes, sir, I do. I know where I stand and what I am doing. But I don't believe I have seen a newspaper. I can't remember. I do not know what I want to know. I cannot remember anything but an utter blank.

So I told the doctor, and told him what I thought of the matter. Then I said: "You will stay here until I return?"

"It was a debt that could not be paid," I said. "It was always a debt that could not be paid." I walked the length of the room, keeping watch out of sight. Then I went to my room. At the door I met Blount. He looked pale and worried. I asked him if he had been disturbed. He looked excited and seemed about to go. When he saw I hesitated he came over to me. "What did I tell you?" he asked. "You don't understand, I hope?"

"No, sir," I answered.

"Well, I'm not going to let him get away. You're going to stay."

He looked at me in astonishment. "Where? How long are you going to stay?"

I didn't answer him; but I felt pretty sure he was telling the truth, so I didn't answer. Then I went back to the desk.

Blount was standing near the window looking after me. "I wonder where that other man is," he said to me.

"I've got one of the same kind of things," I replied. "I haven't been seen since the night you sent for me."

Blount smiled. "That's funny," he said. "But you will not see him again to-night. I want to find the man who killed himself. But you can't do it. You can't do anything till you have found out what that thing has been about. And now you must come to town with me. I can leave in ten minutes. I'll call you up later."

"I'll go down to-morrow."

Blount took me by the shoulder and led me from the house. He seemed very anxious to make good, and he looked worried. He told me he wanted me to go home with him, and that he had been waiting for me to come up, or something like that. I did not see why I should not do as he said.

The next day I went at once to Blount's office. I put on my hat and overcoat and started downstairs. I found Blount in his office. He came out a few moments later, and asked me if I thought it was worth while going home. I told him it was; and then he told me what I had done. I gave him the story of what had happened the night before. He said he would return home in a few days, and would come back in a few weeks. So I left him there.

When I returned, a few moments later, Blount came over and sat down beside me. "Well," I said, "and how are you?"

"Very well," he replied, "we are much better. We can talk business."

"Where is Blount now?"

"He was here last night," I replied.

"Where has he gone?"

"I don't know. But it's a long way off. They say he's been here all night."

Blount opened his eyes wide. "How long have you known Blount?"

"About two months ago," I answered. "I've known him for some time."

"What were you told about?"

Blount made a little grimace.
