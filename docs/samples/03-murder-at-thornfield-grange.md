## Chapter 1: A Body in the Rain

They found Tom Galt at the inn, and by the time I got there the rain had washed away most of what mattered. The first one of their company was a man named Corder, who lived near the place. He had a lot of money, and had been a farmer for many years, and he used to work for half a dozen or so of his own money.

"Now, it's up to you, Tom," said Corder, "but I'll see you through. You're wanted at the house, and you came out here to look after the other fellow. It may be worth your while to try him on, but I don't think it will be worth your while. That is, unless you want some one else."

I was told that he was a New Yorker, and that he was as keen and strong as the devil.

"Well, Tom," said Corder, "you're going to do the same thing over again. There isn't a man in this town that would be more capable than you are."

"What do you mean?" I asked.

"Just what I say," said Corder, with an air of indifference. "The man you know is the head of the house. When he got into my room I heard him talking about you and the other fellow, and how he was trying to get you away from me. If you come to the point, he'll take it that you are the head of it. He knows it, and he knows it, too. It's only natural for a man to make a mistake like that. So we can't help it."

That settled it for me.

In the afternoon I went down to the village to see Corder and his family; but I found out all there was to hear about the man who had been killed, and that Tom Galt, the local councilman, who had been appointed to the case, was dead. The coroner, who had been called to the church before he died, showed up at the inquest, and the jury returned their verdict, which was very little, as far as any one knew.

I was called out of the village then and there, and set down there as one of the most influential men in the city. As I came out of the churchyard, I was surprised by seeing a woman standing on the bench beside the man, who was talking to a man of middle size, whose clothes were white with dust, and whose face was white with exposure. It was all I could tell him from my own observations. He looked as if he had known his way about in life and death.

"Is the coroner here?" asked the coroner.

"No, sir," said the man. "He is not there now. The coroner's orders are to take no action against him, nor shall he be asked to do so unless you can prove your innocence."

The coroner nodded.

"You can go right up to the house, Mr. Corder," he directed. "I'll look after the body."

When I got into the house, I found Mr. Shea sitting up in bed, looking over some books. I told him what I had learned from the coroner, and he went into the study.

She looked at the money, and said: "I am afraid not," but took the money from the pocket. I could find no more than the note she held in her hand. When she went away, I heard her say: "You need have no fear of my being too hard on you, Mr. Corder."

There was a pause, while I sat with my face turned toward the door. Then I asked: "Who are you? What do you want?"

The landlady made an effort to compose herself. "Oh, sir!" she said, "I know you're very kind, but I'm sorry to hear you say that I was wrong. The woman took up the paper which had fallen from the table. She looked at it with a curious smile.

"All," I told her, "except the key. Don't you think it's all a joke? There isn't even a bit of a joke about what happened after the crime wasn't committed."

"Not a joke!" she cried. "Why should there be? My husband left nothing of the kind."

"That's the story. You see, the pawn-ticket was stolen when he went out to Branchport."
