## Chapter 1: The Call

They found Nora Crane at a speakeasy on Front Street, and by the time I got there the rain had washed away most of what mattered. Late as it was, it stopped short, and the town, in its usual quiet way, came to a stop just below where I was. The people were not looking for the morning papers; they seemed to be talking about the crime at the store.

"Gee! this ain't been goin' to be so bad!" said one of them to me. "Them other newspapers never do nothing but talk about it."

I didn't say anything else at that moment, either, but kept my eyes fixed on the door of the store again. It opened off near a lamp-post.

Not a soul had seen the front of the building since last night, but a man standing there, two feet or more behind the man's head, with his hat over his eyes, was staring at the door. There wasn't any sound from within; the street looked deserted, deserted, empty. And then I heard voices raised above me. They were the men who had passed out of sight. They were talking.

I jumped up, got to my feet and walked away.

And all the while, I stood there, thinking of the man I had seen coming into the shop.

And I wondered whether he knew the man, and if he could be found, and I would go up there now. I wanted to go down there, and, if possible, make sure of what I might have discovered before leaving here.

But I wasn't going to do that. The only thing I had was to see that man and get something out of him, and to have a little conversation with him again before I went up. But there wasn't any use in going.

The man was still walking about the block, and seemed to be getting impatient. He pushed me along, and went ahead of me. It wasn't far. I stopped short and waited.

It was very quiet. The fog was thickening, and the rain was beginning to fall. Not a sound. Then I heard voices:

"Hello! Hello!" I said. "What's the matter?"

There wasn't anybody in the room at all; it was empty except the man beside me. I don't know exactly how they knew me, but it seemed to me I must be mistaken for their voices. The other fellow spoke louder, and I thought he looked like one of them. The other man was sitting on his own legs, and the other was standing up. There was no need to ask why that light was burning.

I don't remember having been able to distinguish anything. But they were talking--the men were talking together now. They talked without the slightest noise. And then I got to thinking. There wasn't a word said to me. That is, it wasn't words; it was only the wind blowing through the window.

"How long have you been here?" the man asked.

I didn't say anything.

"When do you expect to start?" he asked.

And it wasn't any use trying to answer him. He was staring at the wall and the man behind me, and the other was whispering to him.

The client sat across from me and said the police had it wrong about Crane. I didn't hear them say any more than I did. I was too old and deaf to talk any more for some time. I felt that I was not alone. I knew that the man was drunk--and that he wasn't sober. I knew that the trouble was all over for the night. I could have sworn that he'd been drinking again. It made it worse, but it got me all right. We walked out of the station yard to where the lights were brightest on the main street. The other fellow stopped beside me while I told the story. I heard them both say: "Let me alone!" and I heard the same man saying: "I am drunk." And he asked me where I was going, and whether I was sober or drunk, and I told him I was drunk--that I was sick, and that I hadn't anything on me but my clothes.

There wasn't any one there. The other fellow couldn't see me if he saw me. There weren't many people about. He didn't know me; he just stared with an air of disgust at a couple of big fellows standing in front of him. One of those fellows came out of the shadows. He took the chair beside me.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"You've given me something that's a little harder than a blind," I said.

He looked at me as though he had just come from a bad, rather bad fit.

"Do you mean by that that you don't know what's good for you?" he asked.

"Yes," I answered; "but I don't know what it is to be rich."

"Are you mad enough to talk about it?" he asked.

"No, I'm not."

"Then what did it matter? It's just what you've done for me." He stopped looking at me again. "It's a pity you've been so quiet," he went on.

"I have been so quiet," I said.

"And you haven't been here long?"

"Not more than half an hour--or ten minutes."

I told him all that I knew about Crane. The man looked surprised and puzzled. He stood up before me and said: "Well, I will." And then he went on with his story:

"My name is David Wickes."

The man who had spoken was coming out of the shadow of a house across the street in front of the hotel.

"That was the man who called on Nora Crane yesterday," he said. "He said she'd been going there often before if anything happened to him. I guess that's right. She said she was going to stay there for the night until morning if the girl hadn't come out. She said that she'd seen Nora come out, and that she had been waiting for her. I don't know whether she saw or heard anything about the matter. That's all I can tell you."

We walked back through the lighted street together. I didn't see what he could do, but he got out his pipe and lit it.

Kade did not want to talk, but I had come a long way to a rooming house and I was in no hurry to leave. So I went on to the place where the men were standing. The room was empty, and I took out my keys and opened them. There were two doors at the end of it. I unlocked one, turned the key in the lock, and was inside the other.

I was in darkness now. It seemed as though I could hear nothing--only that one of the men who had come with me from the office to the elevator, and was not even talking. When I rose up from my chair I looked around me and then around and then over again. And then I found myself looking into a face that was very white and set under the brim of a big hat.

"What the hell!" I whispered hoarsely. "You're the man that came in here?"

The other answered: "Yes, they are," he said. "They haven't been here since morning." He smiled and put the key in his pocket. "And the woman hasn't seen anything yet? What have you got to say for yourself?"

"Nothing," I told him. "That's all."

He looked at me again, and said: "Let me go in there." Then, "She didn't see anything?" he asked. "She said she was going back to the office, but she couldn't be sure if she saw anybody coming out of the building."

"No," I said; "she didn't see anybody. But she can tell us."

"Yes," said the man, and went back to his keys. "Go on."

I said: "Don't let them talk. You know they don't understand."

"But they won't talk," said the man, "because I don't want to talk."

I said: "I don't want to talk about it. When I came out, Nora did not hear me. She wasn't listening."

I said: "Well, then, what do you know about her?"

"She don't have any friends around here," said the man. "We've only a couple or three servants, and they don't like her--or they wouldn't come across with her."

"You're the man that knows about her?" I said.

The man grinned, and said: "All right," then he added, "you stay with her." He got up and began to move toward the elevator.

After that he passed into the elevator, and when I stood in the corridor he looked at me again as though he was not sure whether I had taken my stand there as a fool, or whether he had, instead of coming away, I just wondered if he didn't have a good look at Nora Crane.

But I knew then that he hadn't noticed us because the elevator boy had left the elevator, and that we were so much alike that he didn't notice us when we went out. So I sat there thinking and trying to think. And then, after a long time, it came. There wasn't anything that I could see except the people in the elevator, and so it was that, and there wasn't anything more than that I could get from them, but there wasn't any other things.

It was mud of an unusual colour on a shoe, and I turned it over in my hand for a long time. The next thing I knew was that it looked as if the whole block had been run down and all out of its course. It seemed like some one moving along the ground, and suddenly I heard a voice say:

"See how badly the women are hurt?"

I looked up. A man was standing beside the shoe that was lying on the floor, and I saw him hold up a piece of the torn, which he had evidently dropped. "Well, sir," said the man, "but I thought you'd better come away with me. They're coming down from the top, and they'll be waiting for you."

We drove down to the street, and he was gone. He hadn't been watching the cabs for more than a few seconds, and now he hurried across the yard. Then he caught the elevated, and he jumped for the roof and started down the street. When he came in sight of the house, his face showed white and drawn. The door was open, and he went into the room where the woman stood. She had been standing just outside the door, but she was not looking toward her husband. The man had turned, and was staring at her in a sort of wonder. Her eyes were wide, and her hand trembled a little.

He stopped as he came in, and the woman looked around the room for a long time. When she saw who it was, she said:

"Why, my dear, they have taken Nora away from me! Nora never did anything but laugh; she said she would go away from me."

He didn't look at her again that night, but he moved over to the window and looked out. The man had gone back to the street. I don't know how long he was there before he went up the stairs, but when he got on the top floor, I knew there was no one in the house but Nora and the man. There wasn't any room for a man standing alone in the hall below to sleep without the key in his hand. He hadn't opened it, and there weren't any letters, and all the time he was trying to think what he ought to do.

The only thing that kept him from doing this was that he had found out where they had taken Nora away, and that he would take her away from them. That didn't make any difference. He said to himself that if he ever got in any more trouble or anything, he'd get his deserts, and he'd give her a good run for her money. He had no right to think that because he could see nothing else for himself. He couldn't very well live up to it for a moment, and he wouldn't be able to think of anybody else going with him.

And that was the end of the affair. I did not know, I didn't even know anything about Nora Crane. It was not likely that he'd have called himself a fool or say a woman an outspoken word in public.
